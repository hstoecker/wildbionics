## A cat that exists only on paper

Few ideas from physics are as famous as a cat that is alive and dead at the same time. Yet the cat never existed. **Schrödinger's cat is a thought experiment** – a scene imagined to test an idea, not an experiment anyone carried out. No cat was ever locked in a box.

In 1935, the physicist Erwin Schrödinger published a long essay on "the present situation in quantum mechanics" in the journal *Die Naturwissenschaften*. At the end of its first part, he described what he himself called a quite burlesque case [1](#ref-1){:.cite}. In the same year, Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen had argued that the wave function of quantum mechanics does not give a complete description of physical reality [2](#ref-2){:.cite}. How the mathematics of quantum theory relates to the world we see was a hot topic.

Schrödinger's scene goes like this [1](#ref-1){:.cite}: a cat is shut in a steel chamber together with a device that the cat cannot touch. The device contains a radioactive substance – so little that within one hour perhaps one of its atoms decays, but with equal probability none. If an atom decays, a Geiger counter responds and, through a relay, releases a small hammer that shatters a flask of hydrocyanic acid, a deadly poison. After one hour, the cat is alive if no atom has decayed, and dead if one has.

The trouble starts when the whole system is described with quantum mechanics. For a single atom, it is normal to describe its state as a combination – a **superposition** – of "decayed" and "not decayed". Applied to the whole chamber, the same description contains the living and the dead cat mixed in equal parts [1](#ref-1){:.cite}. Schrödinger did not believe that cats can be half dead. His point was the opposite: a blurred description that works for atoms cannot simply be taken as a picture of reality for everyday objects.

## The thought experiment in four steps

1. **Seal.** The cat, the radioactive sample, the counter, the hammer and the flask of poison are shut in the chamber. No one can look inside [1](#ref-1){:.cite}.
2. **Wait.** Radioactive decay is random. The amount of substance is chosen so that after one hour a decay is exactly as likely as no decay [1](#ref-1){:.cite}.
3. **Couple.** Atom, counter, hammer, flask and cat are linked in a chain: "decayed" goes with "dead", "not decayed" with "alive". Described by quantum mechanics, the chamber is now in one joint superposition of both possibilities.
4. **Open.** Whoever opens the box finds either a living or a dead cat – never both. After one hour, each result has a probability of 50 %.

## Biology lens: why a real cat is never half dead

A living cat is warm, wet and in constant contact with its surroundings. Air molecules bump into its fur, it gives off heat radiation, light scatters from its body, its heart beats and its nerves fire. Every one of these interactions carries a little information about the cat's state out into the environment. Physicists call this process **decoherence**: the environment in effect keeps watching certain properties of an object, and superpositions of the alternatives disappear. According to Wojciech Zurek, many – perhaps all – of the features of our classical everyday world can be explained in this way, and the environment eliminates in particular the "Schrödinger cat states" [3](#ref-3){:.cite}.

How fast does this happen in living tissue? Max Tegmark estimated decoherence times for processes in the brain, such as the firing of nerve cells. He found **10<sup>−13</sup> to 10<sup>−20</sup> s** – far shorter than the 10<sup>−3</sup> to 10<sup>−1</sup> s on which nerve cells work. For the brain, a classical description is therefore appropriate [4](#ref-4){:.cite}. A whole cat is far larger than a nerve cell and just as warm, so by the same reasoning a superposition of "alive" and "dead" could not last for any measurable time.

Where, then, does the quantum world end? Experiments push the boundary further and further. In 1999, Markus Arndt, Anton Zeilinger and colleagues showed wave interference with C<sub>60</sub> molecules – football-shaped cages of 60 carbon atoms that they called "almost a classical body" because of their many internal ways of moving [5](#ref-5){:.cite}. In 2019, Arndt's group demonstrated superposition for molecules heavier than 25,000 atomic mass units, made of up to 2,000 atoms [6](#ref-6){:.cite}. A cat consists of roughly 10<sup>26</sup> atoms (see the mathematics lens) – a gap of more than twenty orders of magnitude. The cat in the box is a thought experiment, and biology explains why it stays one.

## Physics lens: superposition, measurement and decoherence

**Superposition.** In quantum mechanics, the state of a system can be a combination of several possible states. A radioactive nucleus that has had time to decay is described as a superposition of "decayed" and "not decayed". This is not just a statement of ignorance: superpositions can interfere, like waves that reinforce or cancel each other – that is how they differ from a simple "we don't know".

**Coupling.** In Schrödinger's chamber, the atom does not stay alone. Its state is copied through the counter, the relay and the hammer to the flask and the cat. The result is a single joint state in which "decayed" is linked to "dead" and "not decayed" to "alive". Neither the atom nor the cat has a state of its own any more – only the pair has.

**Measurement.** When the box is opened, only one of the two results appears. Quantum theory predicts the probabilities of the results with great accuracy, but how and why a single result arises has been debated since the 1930s. This open question is known as the **measurement problem**, and Schrödinger's cat is its best-known illustration.

**Decoherence.** Part of the answer is well understood today. An object that interacts with its environment loses the ability to show interference between its alternatives: the environment in effect measures it all the time. The superposition does not become visible as a "half-dead cat"; it turns, for all practical purposes, into an ordinary either–or [3](#ref-3){:.cite}. Decoherence explains why we never observe superpositions of everyday objects. Whether it answers every question about measurement is still discussed.

**Cats in the laboratory.** Physicists have built small "Schrödinger cats" to watch this process:

- In 1996, Christopher Monroe, David Wineland and colleagues put a single laser-cooled beryllium ion into a superposition of two separate positions and proved it by detecting the interference between the two wave packets [7](#ref-7){:.cite}.
- In the same year, Michel Brune, Serge Haroche and colleagues created a superposition of a microwave field in a cavity whose phase pointed in two different directions at once – the "meter" of a measuring apparatus showing two readings. They watched this superposition turn into an ordinary mixture step by step as decoherence unfolded [8](#ref-8){:.cite}.

## Mathematics lens: amplitudes, probabilities and a half-life

**Radioactive decay.** For a sample with half-life *T*, the probability that nothing has decayed after the time *t* is

<div class="formula" role="math" aria-label="P no decay equals 2 to the power of minus t over T"><var>P</var><sub>no decay</sub>(<var>t</var>) = 2<sup>−<var>t</var>/<var>T</var></sup></div>

Schrödinger chose the amount of substance so that after one hour a decay is as likely as none [1](#ref-1){:.cite} – in other words, *T* = 1 hour for "at least one decay". After two hours the cat would survive with a probability of 25 %, after three hours with 12.5 %.

**Amplitudes.** Quantum mechanics describes the state by amplitudes, not probabilities. As a shorthand for the whole chamber:

<div class="formula" role="math" aria-label="psi of t equals square root of 2 to the minus t over T times ket alive, plus square root of 1 minus 2 to the minus t over T times ket dead">|<var>ψ</var>(<var>t</var>)⟩ = √(2<sup>−<var>t</var>/<var>T</var></sup>) |alive⟩ + √(1 − 2<sup>−<var>t</var>/<var>T</var></sup>) |dead⟩</div>

The **Born rule** turns amplitudes into probabilities: square their absolute values. After one hour both amplitudes are 1/√2, and (1/√2)<sup>2</sup> = ½ – the 50 % of the thought experiment.

**Superposition or ignorance?** Both a real superposition and a box whose cat is simply alive or dead – we just don't know which – give 50 % for each result when you look. The difference only shows up in a second kind of measurement, one that tests for interference. The **density matrix** makes this visible:

<div class="formula formula--steps"><span><var>ρ</var> = ( ½ &nbsp; <var>c</var> ; <var>c</var> &nbsp; ½ )</span><span>superposition: <var>c</var> = ½ · ignorance: <var>c</var> = 0</span></div>

The diagonal entries are the probabilities of "alive" and "dead". The off-diagonal entry *c* measures the **coherence** – the ability to interfere. A test that asks "is the state (|alive⟩ + |dead⟩)/√2?" answers "yes" with probability ½ + *c*: always for the superposition, only half of the time for mere ignorance. Decoherence makes *c* decay, roughly as *c*(*t*) = ½ e<sup>−*t*/*τ*</sup> with a decoherence time *τ*.

**How big is a cat?** A cat of 4 kg consists mostly of water, whose atoms weigh on average 18 u / 3 ≈ 6 u. Proteins, fat and bone contain heavier atoms, so assume an average of about 7 u. Then the cat contains

<div class="formula formula--steps"><span><var>N</var> ≈ 4 kg / (7 · 1.66 · 10<sup>−27</sup> kg)</span><span>≈ <strong>3 · 10<sup>26</sup></strong> atoms</span></div>

The largest molecules shown in a superposition have up to 2,000 atoms [6](#ref-6){:.cite} – about 10<sup>23</sup> times fewer.

## Computer science lens: telling a superposition from ignorance

The mathematics lens showed that "alive or dead?" cannot tell a superposition from simple ignorance – both give 50 : 50. Only an interference test can. The program below simulates many identical boxes, each opened once, and applies both tests while decoherence gradually destroys the coherence *c*. It works with the density matrix directly and draws every single result at random according to the Born rule. The question: how quickly does the difference between a superposition and an ordinary "either–or" disappear?

In a real laboratory the interference test is done on ions or light fields, not on cats [7](#ref-7){:.cite} [8](#ref-8){:.cite}. The time is measured in units of the decoherence time *τ*.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

BOXES = 1000        # boxes opened per point in time (repetitions of the experiment)
TAU = 1.0           # decoherence time – the unit of time on the x axis
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)


def density_matrix(t):
    """State after time t: the equal superposition (|alive⟩ + |dead⟩)/√2,
    whose coherence – the off-diagonal entry – decays as the environment 'watches'."""
    coherence = 0.5 * np.exp(-t / TAU)
    return np.array([[0.5, coherence],
                     [coherence, 0.5]])


def probabilities(rho):
    """Born rule for two measurements on the same state.
    'Alive or dead?' asks for |alive⟩; the interference test asks for (|alive⟩ + |dead⟩)/√2."""
    p_alive = rho[0, 0]
    p_interference = 0.5 * (rho[0, 0] + rho[1, 1]) + rho[0, 1]
    return p_alive, p_interference


def open_boxes(p):
    """Each box gives one yes/no answer; return the fraction of 'yes' among BOXES boxes."""
    return rng.binomial(BOXES, p) / BOXES


print("time/τ   alive: theory  measured   interference: theory  measured")
for t in [0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5]:
    p_alive, p_int = probabilities(density_matrix(t))
    print(f"{t:6.1f}   {p_alive:13.3f}  {open_boxes(p_alive):8.3f}   {p_int:20.3f}  {open_boxes(p_int):8.3f}")
p_mix = probabilities(np.diag([0.5, 0.5]))[1]
print(f"a box that is simply alive or dead (no superposition): interference {p_mix:.3f}")

times = np.linspace(0, 5, 26)
exact = np.array([probabilities(density_matrix(t)) for t in times])
measured = np.array([[open_boxes(p) for p in row] for row in exact])
plt.rcParams["font.size"] = 13      # large type: the chart stays readable on a phone
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 5.2), layout="constrained")
ax.plot(times, exact[:, 1], lw=2.5, label="interference test (theory)")
ax.plot(times, measured[:, 1], "o", ms=5, label=f"interference test ({BOXES} boxes)")
ax.plot(times, exact[:, 0], "--", lw=2, label="alive or dead? (theory)")
ax.plot(times, measured[:, 0], "s", ms=4, mfc="none", label=f"alive or dead? ({BOXES} boxes)")
ax.axhline(0.5, color="gray", lw=1, ls=":")
ax.text(3.1, 0.54, "no superposition left", color="dimgray", fontsize=11)
ax.set_xlabel("time (in units of the decoherence time τ)")
ax.set_ylabel("fraction of 'yes' answers")
ax.set_title("Only interference reveals the superposition")
ax.set_ylim(0.3, 1.05)
ax.legend(loc="upper right", fontsize=10)
plt.show()
```

What the result teaches:

- **"Alive or dead?" never shows the superposition.** At every time, both theory and simulation give 50 %, whether the coherence is still there or not. Opening the box and looking is exactly the measurement that cannot reveal the superposition.
- **The interference test does.** At the start it gives "yes" in every box (theory 1.000); after one decoherence time only 0.684, after five 0.503 – practically the 0.500 of a box that is simply alive or dead.
- **Single results are random.** With 1,000 boxes, the measured fractions scatter by a few hundredths around the theory. Quantum mechanics predicts probabilities, not the outcome of one box.

Two programming ideas are worth noticing:

- **The density matrix as the data structure.** Four numbers hold everything the program needs: probabilities on the diagonal, coherence off the diagonal. Decoherence is one line – a decaying exponential on the off-diagonal entries.
- **Monte Carlo with `binomial`.** Instead of opening 1,000 boxes one by one, `rng.binomial(BOXES, p)` draws the number of "yes" answers directly – the same statistics, much faster.

Try it yourself – each change takes one line:

- Set `BOXES = 100000`: the measured values move much closer to the theory; the scatter shrinks roughly with the square root of the number of boxes.
- Set `TAU = 0.2`: decoherence is five times faster. After half a time unit the interference test gives only 0.541 instead of 0.803, after one time unit 0.503 – for a large, warm object like a cat, *τ* would be unimaginably shorter still.

## From a paper cat to quantum technology

Schrödinger's cat began as a critique, but "cat states" have become tools. In 2013, a team at Yale University mapped quantum information onto superpositions of two microwave fields in a superconducting cavity – cat states with up to 111 photons [9](#ref-9){:.cite}. In 2020, Raphaël Lescanne and colleagues encoded a qubit in two such states of a superconducting resonator. The further apart the two states were pulled, the rarer one kind of error – the bit-flip – became: it fell exponentially, while the other kind, the phase-flip, grew only linearly [10](#ref-10){:.cite}. Such **cat qubits** are one of the routes towards quantum computers that correct their own errors.

At the same time, experiments with ever larger molecules test whether quantum mechanics holds unchanged for heavier objects [6](#ref-6){:.cite}. And the thought experiment itself remains one of the best ways to think about the boundary between the quantum world and ours – without harming a single cat.
