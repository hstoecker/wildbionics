## Turning over in mid-air

Hold a cat upside down, let it go, and within a fraction of a second it has turned over and lands on its feet. This **air-righting reflex** looks effortless, but it puzzled scientists for a long time. Researchers have studied the falling cat since 1894 [1](#ref-1){:.cite} – because at first sight it seems to break a law of physics.

The problem is **angular momentum**. A falling cat has nothing to push against: no floor, no wall, only air. Without a push, its total angular momentum cannot change. If the cat is released without any spin, its angular momentum is zero – and a rigid body with zero angular momentum cannot start to rotate. Yet the cat rotates by 180°.

The solution is that a cat is not rigid. Mammals right themselves by twisting and bending their spine, which changes how their mass is distributed – their **moment of inertia** [1](#ref-1){:.cite}. In 1969, Thomas Kane and M. P. Scher modelled the cat as two cylinders, the front and the rear half of the body, joined at the waist, and showed that such a body can turn over with zero angular momentum [2](#ref-2){:.cite}. Human divers and trampolinists use the same principle to twist in the air [3](#ref-3){:.cite}.

## The turn in four steps

A simplified version of the cat's manoeuvre, the "tuck and turn" (Fig. 1):

1. **Upside down.** The cat falls with its back towards the ground. Its balance organ in the inner ear tells it which way is down [4](#ref-4){:.cite}.
2. **Front half turns.** The cat pulls in its front legs and twists its front half round. With the legs close to the body, the front half turns easily; the rear half, with stretched hind legs, turns back only a little.
3. **Rear half follows.** Now the cat stretches its front legs and pulls in its hind legs. The rear half turns round, and the front half turns back only a little.
4. **Feet down.** Both halves face the ground, and the cat stretches its legs to land.

At every moment the rotations of the two halves cancel each other – the total angular momentum stays zero. Real cats also bend their body at the waist while they twist [1](#ref-1){:.cite}; Kane and Scher's model of two cylinders includes this bend [2](#ref-2){:.cite}.

## Biology lens: a reflex that grows up – and its limits

Kittens are not born with a working air-righting reflex. It develops in the first weeks of life and is mature by about **33 days**. Cremieux and colleagues compared normal kittens with kittens that had been blind since birth: both developed the reflex at the same pace. The air-righting reflex is therefore mainly controlled by the **vestibular system**, the balance organ in the inner ear [4](#ref-4){:.cite}.

The tail plays a surprisingly small role. Cats with and without a tail right themselves equally well – the turn comes from the spine [1](#ref-1){:.cite}. In geckos it is the other way round: a gecko that falls on its back swings its large tail and turns over in about **106 ms**, the shortest air-righting time reported for animals without wings [1](#ref-1){:.cite}.

The reflex does not make cats safe from falls. Veterinarians call the injuries of cats that fall from buildings **high-rise syndrome**. Of 132 cats treated over five months, 90 % had injuries to the chest; 90 % of the cats that needed treatment survived [5](#ref-5){:.cite}. A study of 119 cats from Zagreb found an average fall height of four storeys; 96.5 % of the cats survived, but 46.2 % had broken limbs, and falls from the seventh floor or higher caused more severe injuries [6](#ref-6){:.cite}. Landing on its feet spreads the impact over the legs, but it does not cancel it.

## Physics lens: angular momentum and a body that changes shape

**Angular momentum** measures how much a body rotates. For a body turning about a fixed axis, it is the product of its moment of inertia *I* and its angular velocity *ω*:

<div class="formula" role="math" aria-label="L equals I times omega"><var>L</var> = <var>I</var> · <var>ω</var></div>

The **moment of inertia** describes how the mass is distributed around the axis: mass far from the axis counts much more than mass close to it. A cat that stretches its legs has a larger moment of inertia than a cat with its legs tucked in.

Angular momentum changes only if a **torque** acts on the body. Gravity pulls on every part of the cat, but in free fall it acts as if on the centre of mass and therefore exerts no torque about it. Air resistance is small during the short time the cat needs to turn. So the cat's angular momentum stays constant – zero, if it is dropped without spin.

How can a body with zero angular momentum turn? For a **rigid** body it cannot: *L* = 0 means *ω* = 0. A cat, however, consists of parts that can rotate against each other. If the front half turns one way, the rear half must turn the other way so that the angular momenta cancel:

<div class="formula" role="math" aria-label="I front times omega front plus I rear times omega rear equals zero"><var>I</var><sub>front</sub> · <var>ω</var><sub>front</sub> + <var>I</var><sub>rear</sub> · <var>ω</var><sub>rear</sub> = 0</div>

The half with the smaller moment of inertia turns more. By tucking in first the front legs and then the hind legs, the cat makes sure that each half turns a lot when it moves forward and only a little when it moves back. After both steps, the whole cat has turned – while *L* was zero at every moment. Divers and trampolinists use the same effect: they can start twists and somersaults during a jump although their angular momentum does not change [3](#ref-3){:.cite}.

## Mathematics lens: how far one cycle turns the cat

Take the two halves of the "tuck and turn" model. A half with tucked legs has the moment of inertia *I*<sub>t</sub>, with stretched legs the larger *I*<sub>e</sub>. The cat twists its halves against each other by an angle *φ*. With zero total angular momentum, the angle *φ* is shared in inverse proportion to the moments of inertia:

<div class="formula formula--steps"><span>Δ<var>θ</var><sub>front</sub> = <var>φ</var> · <span class="frac"><span class="frac__num"><var>I</var><sub>rear</sub></span><span class="frac__den"><var>I</var><sub>front</sub> + <var>I</var><sub>rear</sub></span></span></span><span>Δ<var>θ</var><sub>rear</sub> = −<var>φ</var> · <span class="frac"><span class="frac__num"><var>I</var><sub>front</sub></span><span class="frac__den"><var>I</var><sub>front</sub> + <var>I</var><sub>rear</sub></span></span></span></div>

**Step 1** (front legs tucked, twist by +*φ*): the front half turns by *φ* · *I*<sub>e</sub> / (*I*<sub>t</sub> + *I*<sub>e</sub>). **Step 2** (hind legs tucked, twist back by −*φ*): the front half turns back by only *φ* · *I*<sub>t</sub> / (*I*<sub>t</sub> + *I*<sub>e</sub>). What remains after one cycle – for the front and the rear half alike – is

<div class="formula" role="math" aria-label="delta theta equals phi times I e minus I t over I e plus I t">Δ<var>θ</var> = <var>φ</var> · <span class="frac"><span class="frac__num"><var>I</var><sub>e</sub> − <var>I</var><sub>t</sub></span><span class="frac__den"><var>I</var><sub>e</sub> + <var>I</var><sub>t</sub></span></span></div>

Two results follow directly. If the cat does not change its shape (*I*<sub>e</sub> = *I*<sub>t</sub>), it does not turn at all. And the more the moment of inertia changes, the more each cycle achieves: with *I*<sub>e</sub> = 3 *I*<sub>t</sub> and *φ* = 180°, one cycle turns the cat by 180° · 2/4 = **90°**.

**How far does the cat fall meanwhile?** In free fall, the height after the time *t* is

<div class="formula" role="math" aria-label="h equals one half g t squared"><var>h</var> = ½ <var>g</var> <var>t</var><sup>2</sup></div>

If a turn took 0.3 s – an assumed value for illustration – the cat would drop ½ · 9.81 m/s² · (0.3 s)<sup>2</sup> ≈ **44 cm** before its feet point down. Because *h* grows with the square of the time, a turn that takes twice as long needs four times the height.

## Computer science lens: simulating a turn without spin

Can a body really turn by 180° while its angular momentum is zero at every step? The program below simulates the "tuck and turn" model from the mathematics lens in small time steps. It checks the total angular momentum at every step, counts how many cycles the cat needs to land on its feet and computes how far it falls meanwhile. The moments of inertia and the duration of a twist are **assumed values** chosen for illustration, not measurements.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Two halves of the cat turn about the same axis (the spine), in the "tuck and turn" model.
I_TUCKED = 1.0      # moment of inertia of a half with its legs pulled in (relative units)
I_EXTENDED = 3.0    # the same half with its legs stretched out – assumed 3 times larger
TWIST = 180.0       # how far the front half twists against the rear half in each step (degrees)
STEP_TIME = 0.1     # duration of one twist (s), assumed
G = 9.81            # gravitational acceleration (m/s²)


def twist(i_front, i_rear, d_relative):
    """Twist the halves against each other by d_relative with zero total angular momentum.
    I_f·Δθ_f + I_r·Δθ_r = 0 and Δθ_f − Δθ_r = d_relative give each half's rotation."""
    d_front = d_relative * i_rear / (i_front + i_rear)
    d_rear = -d_relative * i_front / (i_front + i_rear)
    return d_front, d_rear


# One cycle: (1) front legs tucked, twist the front half; (2) rear legs tucked, untwist.
steps = [(I_TUCKED, I_EXTENDED, +TWIST), (I_EXTENDED, I_TUCKED, -TWIST)]
front, rear, time = [0.0], [0.0], [0.0]
largest_momentum = 0.0
for cycle in range(2):
    for i_front, i_rear, d_relative in steps:
        for _ in range(50):                     # each twist in 50 small steps
            d_front, d_rear = twist(i_front, i_rear, d_relative / 50)
            rate_front, rate_rear = d_front / (STEP_TIME / 50), d_rear / (STEP_TIME / 50)
            largest_momentum = max(largest_momentum, abs(i_front * rate_front + i_rear * rate_rear))
            front.append(front[-1] + d_front)
            rear.append(rear[-1] + d_rear)
            time.append(time[-1] + STEP_TIME / 50)

per_cycle = front[100]
print(f"largest total angular momentum during the turn: {largest_momentum:.3f}")
if abs(per_cycle) < 1e-6:
    print("turn per cycle: 0° – without changing shape the cat cannot turn")
else:
    cycles = 180.0 / per_cycle
    fall_time = cycles * 2 * STEP_TIME
    print(f"turn per cycle: {per_cycle:.1f}° (front and rear half alike)")
    print(f"cycles needed to land on its feet (180°): {cycles:.1f}")
    print(f"time needed: {fall_time:.2f} s  →  it falls {0.5 * G * fall_time**2 * 100:.0f} cm meanwhile")

time = np.array(time)
plt.rcParams["font.size"] = 13      # large type: the chart stays readable on a phone
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 5.2), layout="constrained")
for k in range(4):                      # shade the steps in which the front legs are tucked
    if k % 2 == 0:
        ax.axvspan(k * STEP_TIME, (k + 1) * STEP_TIME, color="0.92")
ax.plot(time, front, lw=2.5, label="front half")
ax.plot(time, rear, "--", lw=2.5, label="rear half")
ax.axhline(180, color="gray", lw=1, ls=":")
ax.text(0.005, 186, "feet down (180°)", color="dimgray", fontsize=11)
ax.text(0.205, -52, "grey: front legs\ntucked", color="dimgray", fontsize=11)
ax.set_xlabel("time (s)")
ax.set_ylabel("rotation about the spine (degrees)")
ax.set_title("Changing shape turns the cat – with zero spin")
ax.set_ylim(-60, 230)
ax.legend(loc="lower right")
plt.show()
```

What the result teaches:

- **Zero angular momentum, 180° turn.** The largest total angular momentum during the whole manoeuvre is 0.000 – yet after two cycles both halves have turned by 180°. Each cycle turns the cat by **90.0°**, exactly as the formula of the mathematics lens predicts.
- **Forward far, back a little.** In the chart, each half moves a long way while its own legs are tucked and a short way back while they are stretched. This asymmetry is the whole trick.
- **The model is slower than a cat.** With the assumed 0.1 s per twist, the model needs 0.40 s and falls about 78 cm. Real cats also bend their body and turn in one smooth movement [2](#ref-2){:.cite}, so they need less height.

Two programming ideas are worth noticing:

- **A conservation law as a test.** The program does not just assume that the angular momentum is zero – it computes *I*<sub>front</sub>*ω*<sub>front</sub> + *I*<sub>rear</sub>*ω*<sub>rear</sub> at every step and reports the largest value. Checking a physical law inside the simulation catches mistakes early.
- **Guarding against division by zero.** If the halves never change shape, the turn per cycle is zero and "cycles needed" would be infinite. The program detects this case and says so instead of printing nonsense.

## Physical AI lens: robots that turn in mid-air

A robot that jumps, falls or floats in space faces the cat's problem: it has nothing to push against, yet it must land the right way up. Two solutions from biology are being copied.

**An active tail.** Geckos turn over in the air by swinging their tail; this observation inspired an active tail for a climbing robot [1](#ref-1){:.cite}. Thomas Libby, Robert Full and colleagues showed that leaping lizards swing their tails to redirect angular momentum from the body to the tail. They built a lizard-sized robot with an active tail that used sensory feedback to keep its body level as it drove off a ramp [8](#ref-8){:.cite}.

**Legs instead of a tail.** Nikita Rudin, Marco Hutter and colleagues trained a neural network with **deep reinforcement learning** to control the jumping four-legged robot SpaceBok – using only its legs to turn the body in mid-air, as a cat does. The policies were trained in simulation for low-gravity celestial bodies and then transferred to the real robot on a test bed for two-dimensional microgravity experiments, where it jumped and landed again and again [9](#ref-9){:.cite}.

For Physical AI, the falling cat teaches that moving the body can itself be the control. No motor pushes against the ground, no thruster fires – shifting mass at the right moment is enough to change orientation. Learning systems like SpaceBok's controller discover such movement sequences by trial and error in simulation, much as a kitten needs a few weeks to master the reflex [4](#ref-4){:.cite}.

## From cats to divers and robots

The falling cat shows that conservation laws limit what a body can do, but not how cleverly it can move within those limits. A body that changes its shape can reorient itself without any external help – a principle shared by cats, geckos [1](#ref-1){:.cite}, divers [3](#ref-3){:.cite} and legged robots [9](#ref-9){:.cite}.

Open questions remain. How the nervous system coordinates the many joints of the spine and legs so quickly is still being modelled [7](#ref-7){:.cite}. And for cats themselves the best protection is simple: the reflex helps, but secured windows and balconies prevent the injuries of high-rise syndrome [5](#ref-5){:.cite} [6](#ref-6){:.cite}.
