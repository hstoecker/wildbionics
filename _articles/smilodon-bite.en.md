## A cat with sabres

*Smilodon fatalis* is the most famous of the sabre-toothed cats. Despite its popular name, the "sabre-toothed tiger" was not a tiger: ancient DNA shows that the sabre-toothed cats (Machairodontinae) form a group that is distinct from all living cats, and that *Smilodon* split from its sabre-toothed relative *Homotherium* about 18 million years ago [1](#ref-1){:.cite}.

Most of what we know about *Smilodon fatalis* comes from an unusual place: the asphalt seeps of Rancho La Brea in the middle of Los Angeles. Their fossils span the past ~50,000 years [2](#ref-2){:.cite}. Dying plant-eaters stuck in the asphalt lured predators, which could become trapped in turn – and so predators far outnumber plant-eaters among the fossils [3](#ref-3){:.cite}. Among the 3,324 fossil records analysed in one study, 51 % were dire wolves and 33 % *Smilodon* [3](#ref-3){:.cite}.

Its upper canines are among the most specialised teeth known [4](#ref-4){:.cite}. In the specimens studied by Zhijie Jack Tseng, they reached a fully erupted height of 100–140 mm [4](#ref-4){:.cite}. Yet the bite that drove them was surprisingly weak [5](#ref-5){:.cite}. This article follows the clues – bones, mechanics and chemistry – to how such an animal killed.

## The kill in four steps

The following sequence is the reconstruction favoured by several studies; parts of it are debated, as the lenses show.

1. **Ambush.** *Smilodon* is regarded as an ambush predator, and its injuries – frequent in the lower back and shoulders – point to a risky way of killing [6](#ref-6){:.cite}.
2. **Hold.** Powerful forelimbs pinned the prey and limited its struggles, protecting the long canines from breaking [7](#ref-7){:.cite}.
3. **Bite.** With the prey restrained, the cat bit – most likely into the neck – with force from the neck muscles adding to the relatively weak jaw muscles [5](#ref-5){:.cite}.
4. **Kill quickly.** The relatively low bite force may reflect an ability to kill large prey quickly, without the long suffocating bite of a lion [5](#ref-5){:.cite}.

## Biology lens: built to hold

**Forelimbs like a wrestler.** Julie Meachen-Samuels and Blaire Van Valkenburgh measured the bones of *Smilodon*, 28 living cat species and the even larger American lion *Panthera atrox* on radiographs. The upper arm bones of *Smilodon* were reinforced by thicker bone walls than in any living cat or in the American lion; the thigh bone was thickened too, but within the range of other cats. The authors interpret this as a greater ability to subdue prey with the forelimbs – to keep the canines from breaking and to position the bite for a quick kill [7](#ref-7){:.cite}.

**What injuries reveal.** Caitlin Brown and colleagues studied over 1,900 diseased or injured bones from Rancho La Brea. Traumatic injuries were more frequent in *Smilodon* (4.3 %) than in the dire wolf (2.8 %), which implies that its way of killing carried a greater risk of injury. *Smilodon*, which the authors classify as an ambush predator, was injured more often than expected in the lumbar vertebrae and the shoulders; the dire wolf, a pursuit predator, more often in the limbs and the neck vertebrae [6](#ref-6){:.cite}.

**Alone or in groups?** Chris Carbone and colleagues compared the fossil counts at La Brea with predators that come to recordings of distressed prey in Africa. In both, social species dominate – lions and spotted hyenas in Africa, dire wolves and *Smilodon* at La Brea – and the authors conclude that *Smilodon* was social [3](#ref-3){:.cite}. Christian Kiffner questioned the comparison: in the asphalt seeps, heavier animals were more likely to get stuck, the lure lasted longer than a playback, and the Ice Age predators of North America need not have behaved like those of an African savannah [8](#ref-8){:.cite}.

**A family in Ecuador.** Coastal deposits in Ecuador preserved at least two young *Smilodon fatalis* and one adult, probably killed together. A tooth (the third premolar) present in both young suggests inheritance – a rare sign of relatedness in the fossil record. The young were at least two years old and were found with an adult that was probably their mother, which indicates prolonged parental care. According to Ashley Reynolds and colleagues, *Smilodon* grew about as fast as a tiger, but for as long as a lion [9](#ref-9){:.cite}.

## Physics lens: a weak bite, a strong neck

**Bite force.** Colin McHenry and colleagues built one of the most detailed computer models of a vertebrate skull and simulated how *Smilodon* bit. The bite force produced by its jaw muscles was relatively weak – about **one third** of that of a lion of comparable size – and the skull withstood forces from struggling prey poorly. It was better suited to bites on restrained prey, in which the neck muscles add to the force of the bite. The authors conclude that prey was brought to the ground and held before the killing bite, which was most likely directed at the neck [5](#ref-5){:.cite}. Comparing many living and fossil predators, Stephen Wroe and colleagues likewise found a relatively low bite force for its body mass [10](#ref-10){:.cite}.

**A stiff skull for a stab.** Borja Figueirido and colleagues compared the amount of compact and spongy bone in the skulls of *Smilodon* and the scimitar-toothed cat *Homotherium*. *Smilodon* had an extremely thick skull with relatively little spongy bone, consistent with a large investment in cranial strength for a stabbing "canine-shear" bite. *Homotherium* had more spongy bone and probably killed more like a lion, with a clamp-and-hold bite [11](#ref-11){:.cite}.

**How wide, how hard?** From the bony attachment sites of the jaw muscles, Ashley Deutsch and colleagues estimate a bite force of about **1,280 N** at the canine and about **4,670 N** at the carnassial, the shearing cheek tooth. That is similar to estimates for the much smaller jaguar, not for the largest living cats. The angle to which *Smilodon* could open its jaw was significantly larger than in living cats, but the distance between the canine tips was not [12](#ref-12){:.cite}.

**An alternative model.** Not everyone accepts the neck-powered bite. In a study with a digitised replica of the skull and neck, Jeffrey Brown found that rotating the skull with the neck muscles does not close the jaws. He proposes instead that the lower jaw was braced against the neck of the prey while the forelimbs rotated the skull forward, like a lever [13](#ref-13){:.cite}.

**Many sabres, many solutions.** Sabre-like canines evolved several times in different groups of vertebrates over the last 265 million years. Simulations of gape angle, bending strength and bite force by Stephan Lautenschlager and colleagues show a remarkably high functional diversity: different sabre-toothed lineages evolved different ways of killing [14](#ref-14){:.cite}.

## Mathematics lens: the sabre as a beam

**A tooth is a cantilever.** A canine is fixed in the jaw at one end and free at the other – a **cantilever beam**. Blaire Van Valkenburgh and Christopher Ruff modelled canines in this way, with an elliptical cross-section, and found that the canines of sabre-toothed cats resemble those of living dogs more than those of living cats in shape and strength [15](#ref-15){:.cite}. Tseng used the same beam model and computer simulations to follow the sabre of *Smilodon* through its eruption [4](#ref-4){:.cite}.

A force *F* at the tip of a tooth of height *h* bends it with the moment *M* = *F* · *h*. The largest stress occurs at the base, on the surface farthest from the bending axis:

<div class="formula" role="math" aria-label="sigma equals M over W">σ = <span class="frac"><span class="frac__num"><var>M</var></span><span class="frac__den"><var>W</var></span></span></div>

Here *W* is the section modulus, which describes how well the cross-section resists bending. For an ellipse with semi-axis *a* front to back and *b* side to side, standard beam theory gives (a calculation for this article):

<div class="formula" role="math" aria-label="W side equals pi times a times b squared over 4, W front equals pi times b times a squared over 4"><var>W</var><sub>side</sub> = π · <var>a</var> · <var>b</var><sup>2</sup> / 4 &nbsp;·&nbsp; <var>W</var><sub>front</sub> = π · <var>b</var> · <var>a</var><sup>2</sup> / 4</div>

**The weak direction.** The ratio of the two stresses for the same force is simply *a*/*b*: a tooth twice as long as it is wide is stressed twice as much from the side as from the front. For the fully erupted canine of specimen AMNH FM55576, the cross-section at the gum line measures 32.17 mm by 18.00 mm [4](#ref-4){:.cite} – so a sideways load stresses it about **1.8 times** more than a load from the front. This is why a struggling prey that twists against the sabres is dangerous for the cat.

**Growing into strength.** Tseng found that the cross-section of the sabre grows with its height in a way that increases its bending strength, although it bends more easily sideways as it grows. For more than half of the eruption period, the milk canine and the permanent canine stood side by side, which effectively widened the tooth and brought strength and stiffness closer to theoretical optima [4](#ref-4){:.cite}.

## Chemistry lens: what teeth remember

**A clock in the enamel.** Tooth enamel is formed layer by layer, and it locks in the ratio of the oxygen isotopes ¹⁸O and ¹⁶O, written δ¹⁸O, in the body when it forms. Measured along a growing canine, the changes in this ratio can be used as a clock for how fast the enamel grew. For the sabre-toothed cat *Smilodon gracilis*, Robert Feranec found a growth rate of about **5 mm per month** over roughly 16 months [16](#ref-16){:.cite}. For *Smilodon fatalis*, Aleksander Wysocki and colleagues combined a previously published oxygen-isotope growth rate with CT scans of developing teeth: the upper canine erupted at about **6.0 mm per month**. The rest of the permanent teeth were in place by 14–22 months of age, but the sabres finished erupting only at about **34–41 months** – a delayed dental development compared with living cats of similar size [17](#ref-17){:.cite}.

**A menu in the enamel.** Carbon isotopes (δ¹³C) record the plants at the base of the food chain. In the canine of *Smilodon gracilis* from Florida, the carbon isotope values point to prey that fed only on so-called C3 plants; this rules out some prey animals, and the values may indicate prey caught in a closed habitat, according to Feranec [16](#ref-16){:.cite}.

**Competition – a debate.** At Rancho La Brea, Larisa DeSantis and colleagues used several independent lines of evidence from hundreds of carnivore and herbivore fossils. They conclude that dire wolves ate prey from more open habitats than the cats, and that sabre-toothed cats and dire wolves competed little for prey – overturning an earlier view [2](#ref-2){:.cite}. Blaire Van Valkenburgh and colleagues challenged this conclusion, arguing that a lack of competition cannot be inferred from the dental enamel data [18](#ref-18){:.cite}; DeSantis and colleagues replied to these concerns [19](#ref-19){:.cite}. The question remains open.

**Radiocarbon and the end.** Radiocarbon dating uses the decay of the radioactive isotope carbon-14 in once-living material. Robin O'Keefe and colleagues obtained 172 new radiocarbon dates from megafauna at Rancho La Brea. Seven species of extinct large mammals disappeared there by 12,900 years ago. The authors link this to a shift of the ecosystem after aridification and vegetation change, with large fires as the primary cause and growing human impact as a possible trigger – a hypothesis supported by time-series models [20](#ref-20){:.cite}.

                 (54.85, 26.80, 11.96), (65.21, 29.56, 13.25), (75.16, 31.41, 13.94),
                 (85.12, 33.21, 14.94), (95.03, 34.63, 15.65)],
}
SPECIMEN_INDEX = 0          # 0: AMNH FM55576, the most complete eruption series (up to 138 mm); 1: KU S2255
SPECIMEN = list(CANINES)[SPECIMEN_INDEX]
LOAD_N = 100.0              # force at the tip (N) – an assumed round value for comparison
ROUND = False               # True: same cross-sectional area, but a round tooth


def bending_stress(force, height, x, y):
    """Peak bending stress (MPa) at the base of an elliptical cantilever.
    Semi-axes a = x/2 (front-to-back) and b = y/2 (side-to-side), all in mm.
    Side load bends about the long axis: section modulus π·a·b²/4.
    Front load bends about the short axis: section modulus π·b·a²/4."""
    a, b = x / 2, y / 2
    moment = force * height                      # N·mm
    side = moment / (np.pi * a * b**2 / 4)       # N/mm² = MPa
    front = moment / (np.pi * b * a**2 / 4)
    return side, front


data = np.array(CANINES[SPECIMEN])
height, x, y = data.T
if ROUND:                                        # same area, circular: x = y = √(x·y)
    x = y = np.sqrt(x * y)
side, front = bending_stress(LOAD_N, height, x, y)

print(f"{SPECIMEN}, load {LOAD_N:.0f} N at the tip")
for h, s, f in ((height[0], side[0], front[0]), (height[-1], side[-1], front[-1])):
    print(f"  height {h:.0f} mm: {s:.1f} MPa from the side, {f:.1f} MPa from the front")
print(f"fully erupted, a side load stresses the tooth {side[-1] / front[-1]:.1f} times more than a front load")
print(f"from first to last stage, the side stress changes by a factor of {side[-1] / side[0]:.2f}, "
      f"while the height grows {height[-1] / height[0]:.1f} times")

plt.rcParams["font.size"] = 13      # large type: the chart stays readable on a phone
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 5.6), layout="constrained")
ax.plot(height, side, "o-", lw=2.5, color="#c0392b", label="load from the side")
ax.plot(height, front, "s--", lw=2.5, color="#1f6f8b", label="load from the front")
ax.set_ylim(0, 1.15 * side.max())
ax.axvspan(20, 40, color="grey", alpha=0.15)
ax.text(30, 1.12 * side.max(), "typical\ncat canine", ha="center", va="top", fontsize=11)
ax.set_xlabel("erupted height of the canine (mm)")
ax.set_ylabel(f"bending stress at the base (MPa)\nfor {LOAD_N:.0f} N at the tip")
ax.set_xlim(15, 145)
ax.set_title("Sideways, the sabre is the weak spot")
ax.legend(loc="lower right", fontsize=11)
plt.show()

What the result teaches:

- **Sideways is the weak direction.** For the fully erupted canine, a load from the side causes 13.5 MPa, a load from the front 7.5 MPa – about **1.8 times** less. This holds at every stage, because the ratio is just the ratio of the tooth's length to its width.
- **A longer sabre is not a weaker sabre.** The tooth grows 3.4 times taller, yet the side stress falls by a factor of 0.74: the cross-section grows fast enough to more than compensate for the longer lever. This matches Tseng's finding that the cross-section is optimised for bending strength [4](#ref-4){:.cite}.
- **Robust and limited.** The direction effect follows from the measured shape alone. The absolute numbers depend on the assumed load and ignore the jaw bone and the milk canine that supported the growing tooth.

Try it yourself:

- Another specimen: set `SPECIMEN_INDEX = 1` (KU S2255). Its tooth is even flatter; fully erupted, the side load causes 2.2 times the stress of the front load, and from the first to the last stage the side stress falls by a factor of 0.49.
- A round sabre: set `ROUND = True` – the same cross-sectional area, but circular. The direction no longer matters (ratio 1.0), and the side stress of the fully erupted tooth drops to 10.1 MPa.

## What we still do not know

Bones, teeth and chemistry agree on a picture of a cat that held its prey before it bit – but how exactly the bite worked is still debated, between a neck-powered canine-shear bite [5](#ref-5){:.cite} [11](#ref-11){:.cite} and a forelimb-powered lever [13](#ref-13){:.cite}. Whether *Smilodon* hunted in groups [3](#ref-3){:.cite} [8](#ref-8){:.cite}, and how much it competed with dire wolves [2](#ref-2){:.cite} [18](#ref-18){:.cite}, remain open questions.

New tools keep adding evidence: computer models that test how sabre-toothed skulls of different lineages worked [14](#ref-14){:.cite}, chemical clocks in the enamel [17](#ref-17){:.cite}, and ever more precise radiocarbon dates that tie the disappearance of the Ice Age giants at La Brea to a changing, burning landscape [20](#ref-20){:.cite}.
